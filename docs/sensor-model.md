# Probabilistic sensors under realistic telemetry

This document describes how `Reinforce::Environments::MultiCluster` models
what an orchestrator knows about its clusters, and how the probabilistic
sensors of `Reinforce::Shields::MultiClusterSafety` turn that knowledge into
the probability that an allocation fits.

## 1. What makes the free resources uncertain

An orchestrator that allocates services across clusters never sees their
free resources directly. It sees monitoring data, and monitoring works in a
specific way:

- **Clusters are scraped periodically, in time.** A Prometheus-like system
  reads each cluster every few seconds (15 s is a common default) whatever
  the rate of decisions, so at a load peak many decisions rely on the same
  reading, and in a quiet period few do.
- **A scrape reads all of a cluster's metrics at once.** The readings of
  one cluster's CPU, memory, GPU and bandwidth have the same age.
- **Readings arrive late.** In a federation, member clusters ship their
  metrics to a central store, which adds an ingestion latency; scrapes also
  fail now and then, and the last reading stays in use.
- **The orchestrator knows its own decisions.** It knows what it deployed
  and which of its services ended (the Kubernetes scheduler and Karmada
  both account for their own pending allocations), so a stale reading can
  be corrected for everything the orchestrator itself did since.

What remains uncertain is the part of the state the orchestrator does *not*
control: other tenants, other schedulers, in-cluster autoscaling. The model
below makes that the source of uncertainty.

## 2. The testbed

### Time and workload

Time is measured in seconds. The scripted workload
(`MultiCluster::Workloads::Scripted`) generates:

- **Agent requests**, arriving as a Poisson process whose rate follows a
  *profile* λ(t), in requests per second. Each request asks for R
  replicas, with per-replica demands and an optional dependency, drawn
  from request classes. Each decision happens at the arrival of a request.
  Deployed replicas hold their resources for the request's lifetime
  (log-normal), then give them back.
- **Background tenants**, per cluster: services that arrive and depart on
  the cluster by themselves, following their own profile, demands and
  lifetimes. They are deployed when they fit (and dropped otherwise, which
  is counted but not charged to the agent). The agent sees them only
  through telemetry.

A profile is a sequence of segments, written in Ruby or loaded from YAML:

```ruby
profile = MultiCluster::Profile.build do
  constant 0.8, duration: 600            # requests per second
  ramp to: 1.6, duration: 300
  sine mean: 1.0, amplitude: 0.5, period: 1800, duration: 3600
  spike peak: 4.0, at: 900, width: 60    # a flash crowd, added on top
  trace "traces/day.csv", column: "rate", scale: 0.5
  noise sigma: 0.2, correlation: 120     # multiplicative, log-normal
end
```

Arrivals are generated by thinning (Lewis and Shedler) against the
profile's maximum rate.

### Telemetry

A `Telemetry` configuration models a Prometheus-like pipeline:

- each cluster is **scraped** every Δ seconds (default 15), at its own
  random phase and with a small jitter; a scrape reads all the cluster's
  resources at once;
- a reading becomes **available** after an ingestion latency L (default
  log-normal with a median of 2 s);
- a scrape **fails** with probability p (default 0), and the previous
  reading stays in use, getting older.

At decision time t, the orchestrator uses, for each cluster c, the most
recent reading y_c with scrape time s_c and s_c + L ≤ t. Its **age** is
a_c = t − s_c.

### The ledger

The orchestrator knows its own changes to each cluster: the replicas it
deployed and the ones that departed (it watches its own workloads, and
control-plane events are fast compared with metric scrapes). The **ledger**
ℓ_c is the net amount of each resource the agent's services took on
cluster c from s_c on: deployments minus departures. It is exact.

The **assumed free** amount is y_c − ℓ_c, clipped to [0, K_c] (K_c is the
capacity): the reading corrected by everything the agent did since. What
remains unknown is the net consumption of the background tenants over the
age a_c.

### What the agent observes

The observation's free-resource components are the assumed free amounts
(normalized by capacity), and four groups are added per cluster:

- the **age** of its reading, a_c / a_max, clipped to 1;
- the estimated **drift** of background consumption per resource (see
  section 3), scaled by a_max / K and mapped from [−1, 1] to [0, 1];
- the estimated **volatility** per resource, σ̂ √a_max / K, clipped to 1;
- per resource, the estimated probability that the other tenants change
  nothing over a_max, **quiet** = exp(−λ̂ a_max).

All four are information a real orchestrator has: reading timestamps come
with the metrics, and the estimates come from the readings it has already
received. Without the ledger (`ledger: false`, for ablations), the free
components are the raw readings.

## 3. The sensors

### Background consumption over the age of a reading

Take the background tenants of a cluster as an M/G/∞ system: services
arrive at rate λ_b, live for a time with survival function S(u) and mean
m, and each takes D_r of resource r. In steady state, the change of their
consumption over a window of length a is the difference of two
independent compound Poisson sums (the arrivals of the window still alive
at its end, and the departures in the window of the services alive at its
start), each with mean count λ_b ∫₀ᵃ S(u) du. Hence

    E[ΔB_r] = 0,    Var[ΔB_r] = 2 λ_b E[D_r²] ∫₀ᵃ S(u) du ≈ 2 λ_b E[D_r²] a   for a ≪ m.

So, for readings much younger than the services' lifetimes, the unknown
part of the free amount behaves like a diffusion: variance linear in the
age. When the rate changes (a ramp, a daily cycle), a drift appears.

That is the right shape for many small changes. When the other tenants'
services are few and large, though, the change over a short age is
usually *nothing at all*, and occasionally a jump: a normal distribution
with the right variance then spreads probability that really sits on "no
change", and the sensors come out pessimistic (section 5). Not every
service needs every resource either (most need no GPU). The sensors
therefore use a **zero-inflated** model per cluster and resource: over the
age a, the other tenants change resource r with a Poisson rate λ_r, so

- with probability p0 = exp(−λ_r a), nothing changed: ΔB_r = 0;
- otherwise ΔB_r ~ N(m, s²), with m and s² chosen so that the mixture has
  mean v_r a and variance σ_r² a:
  m = v_r a / (1 − p0) and s² = (σ_r² a + (v_r a)²) / (1 − p0) − m²
  (clipped at 0).

### Estimating drift, volatility and rate

v, σ and λ are not assumed known: the environment's monitoring estimates
them from consecutive readings, as an operator could. Between readings
k − 1 and k of a cluster, the background's consumption of each resource
increased by

    r_k = (y_{k−1} − y_k) − (own net consumption in [s_{k−1}, s_k)),

over Δ_k = s_k − s_{k−1}. Exponential moving averages give
v̂ ← EWMA(r_k / Δ_k), σ̂² ← EWMA((r_k − v̂ Δ_k)² / Δ_k) and the fraction
of quiet intervals q̂ ← EWMA([r_k = 0]), from which λ̂ = −ln q̂ / Δ̄ (Δ̄ the
mean interval). The priors are zero drift, a configurable volatility, and
q̂ = 0 (a change is always assumed until intervals without one are seen,
which makes the model purely Gaussian at first). Readings at 0 or at the
capacity are censored, which biases the volatility down on idle or
saturated clusters.

### Fit probability

At decision time, with the assumed free amount y − ℓ of resource r on
cluster c, the probability that the cluster has at least x units free is

    p(x) = 1                                                       if x ≤ 0,
           0                                                       if x > K,
           p0 [y − ℓ ≥ x] + (1 − p0) (1 − Φ((x − (y − ℓ − m)) / s))  otherwise

(the indicator y − ℓ − m ≥ x in place of the normal part when s = 0),
where Φ is the standard normal distribution function: the mixture above,
censored to [0, K]. It is differentiable in x through the normal part, and
so in the allocation shares, which the shield's correction search and the
safety loss need.

The other sensors are built on p:

- **fit** f_c(z) = ∏_r p_{c,r}(z b_r), for z replicas with per-replica
  demand b;
- **comfortable** h_c = f_c(z / κ) / f_c(z): the allocation leaves at least
  1 − κ of the free resources, given that it fits;
- **reject_safe**: the fraction of the rejected replicas that the clusters'
  estimated capacity (the most replicas each hosts with fit probability at
  least τ_cap, by bisection) could not host anyway.

### Assumptions

- **Independence across resources.** f_c is a product, but a background
  service consumes CPU and memory together, so their increments are
  correlated, and "nothing changed" is partly one event for the whole
  cluster. Counting it once per cluster instead of per resource made
  calibration *worse* in our testbed, because most services need no GPU and
  the GPU is usually the binding resource; per-resource rates won.
- **Symmetric changes.** Given a change, the normal part is symmetric
  around the drift, while a change is either an arrival (less free) or a
  departure (more): the remaining pessimism of section 5 comes from there.
- **Stationarity within a window.** Drift, volatility and rate are taken
  constant over the age of a reading.

## 4. Why the sensors must be calibrated

A probability is *calibrated* when the events it predicts happen as often
as it says: among the allocations the sensors give a 0.7 chance of
fitting, about 70% fit. Calibration is not a detail of the sensors; the
whole shield relies on it, for six reasons.

1. **The threshold is a probability.** The shield executes an allocation
   when its safety score reaches τ (0.6 by default), and τ is meant to be
   read as a risk level: "accept at least a 60% chance that the replicas
   land safely". That reading, and any choice of τ an operator makes from
   it, only holds if the probabilities behind the score are calibrated.
   Otherwise τ is just a knob to be tuned by trial and error, for every
   workload.
2. **Both kinds of error cost something.** Pessimistic sensors make the
   shield intervene on allocations that would have fitted: it rejects
   replicas that could have been served, or moves them to looser clusters,
   at a higher cost and latency and with more fragmentation. Optimistic
   sensors let unsafe allocations through, and every overflow is a failed
   deployment.
3. **The safety score compounds errors.** The safety formula computes
   exactly the probability that an allocation lands safely *if* its inputs
   are probabilities. A bias in the inputs propagates and grows: f_c is a
   product over four resources, so a 10% overestimate of each makes a 46%
   overestimate of f_c; the comfort sensor is a ratio of two fit
   probabilities; and reject_safe compares fit probabilities with τ_cap to
   estimate capacity, so a bias moves the capacity estimate itself.
4. **The policy learns the sensors' bias.** The safety loss trains the
   actor toward the allocations the sensors deem safe. With pessimistic
   sensors, the actor learns to reject or spread more than needed, and
   keeps doing so when the shield is removed; with optimistic ones, it
   learns that risky allocations are fine.
5. **Calibration tests the model of the other tenants.** The diffusion and
   the zero-inflation are assumptions about workloads we do not control.
   Calibration is how we check them, and it changed the model twice (from
   a plain normal to a zero-inflated one, and from per-cluster to
   per-resource rates; section 5). In production, workloads drift, and a
   rising calibration error is the signal to re-estimate the model or
   retrain the sensors.
6. **The evidence.** In the PPO-LTN benchmark on the monitored testbed (4
   clusters, 3 seeds, 100k training steps, 20 test episodes), with the
   ledger the sensors' expected calibration error on the learned agents'
   allocations was about 0.01. The shield intervened on about one step in
   ten at test time, and improved both overflow and reward: PPO-LTN 4.0%
   overflow and a reward of 599 against PPO's 4.8% and 587; SAC-LTN 2.9%
   and 611 against SAC's 5.5% and 596. Without the ledger, the error rose
   to 0.05–0.07, and the shield barely helped PPO (4.6% overflow against
   4.9%) and made SAC worse (5.9% against 5.4%).

Calibration is not the whole story, though. A sensor that always predicts
the average fit rate is perfectly calibrated and useless: it cannot tell a
good allocation from a bad one. The Brier score accounts for both
calibration and this discrimination (it decomposes into a calibration term,
a resolution term and the uncertainty of the outcomes themselves), so we
report both. And calibration depends on the
decisions it is measured on: it must be measured on the allocations the
agent actually makes. Greedy best fit, which picks the tightest cluster
that fits, is the hardest case; an oracle that chooses with the true state
(information the sensors lack) makes allocations whose probabilities look
poorly calibrated (an error of about 0.10) simply because of what it
selects.

## 5. Measuring calibration

`Shields::Calibration` collects, for every cluster that receives replicas,
the predicted fit probability f_c(z_c) and whether the batch actually
fitted, and reports:

- the **Brier score**, the mean squared difference between predicted
  probability and outcome (0 is perfect);
- the **expected calibration error** (ECE): predictions binned by
  probability, the mean absolute gap between each bin's mean prediction and
  its observed frequency, weighted by the bin's size;
- the **reliability table** itself (bins, mean prediction, frequency,
  count), for plots.

`MultiClusterSafety#fit_probabilities` gives the sensors' fit probability
per cluster for an observation and allocation shares, and the PPO-LTN
benchmark (`examples/ppo_ltn_multi_cluster.rb`) reports both measures for
every agent's test allocations.

Measured while developing the sensors, on 5 episodes of 1000 decisions, 4
clusters, with greedy best fit and with uniformly random actions:

| Sensors | Greedy overflow | Greedy ECE | Random ECE |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plain normal | 1.7% | 0.087 | 0.017 |
| Zero-inflated, one quiet rate per cluster | 1.7% | 0.047 | 0.012 |
| **Zero-inflated, one quiet rate per resource** | **1.7%** | **0.025** | **0.006** |
| Zero-inflated per resource, without the ledger | 18.7% | 0.207 | 0.028 |

- With the ledger, best fit on the observation almost never overflows: what
  is left comes from the other tenants. Without it, overflow and
  miscalibration come back.
- The zero-inflated, per-resource model is well calibrated; with greedy, it
  is still somewhat pessimistic between 0.5 and 0.8 (e.g. 0.66 predicted,
  0.78 observed). A test (`test/reinforce/shields/calibration.rb`) keeps
  the expected calibration error under 0.03 for random actions.

## 6. Configuration

| Setting | Free components of the observation | Sensors |
|---|---|---|
| `telemetry:` (ledger on, the default) | assumed free (reading − ledger), plus age, drift, volatility and quiet | zero-inflated Gaussian, censored |
| `telemetry:` with `ledger: false` | raw readings, plus age, drift, volatility and quiet | the same (no ledger correction) |

`MultiCluster.monitored` is the preset for this model: clusters with 200
CPUs, 400 memory units, 8 GPUs and 4000 bandwidth units each; agent
arrivals at 1 per second on average along a 600 s cycle with noise,
offering half the capacity; background tenants occupying 35% of each
cluster with services living about 120 s; and telemetry every 15 s with a
2 s ingestion latency and the ledger. Its `load:`, `background_load:`,
`workload:` and `telemetry:` options override the defaults.

## 7. Later

- **Learnt groundings.** Keep the rules of the safety formula fixed and
  learn the fit predicate: a small network trained on (observation, true
  free amount) pairs, which the simulator provides, compared with the
  analytic sensor through calibration.
- **One-sided jumps.** Model other tenants' arrivals and departures
  separately (a change is one or the other), which addresses the remaining
  pessimism.
- **Correlated resources** (section 3).
- **Knowledge of background tenants' plans** (e.g. scheduled batch jobs),
  as an extra input to the drift.
